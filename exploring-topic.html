Exploring a Topic

Topic orientation

Before you decide which specific direction to take with your literature report, it helps to first get a general view of the subject. In this stage, you explore your topic to learn the most important ideas that appear in the field, become familiar with the terminology, and notice possible sub areas. The goal is orientation and overview, not final decisions.

1. Basic explanation

Topic exploration helps you build a conceptual landscape. You are mapping what the topic contains: key concepts, common terms, broad theories or views, and typical angles (for example, mechanisms, interventions, populations, or settings).

At this stage, overview reading is enough. You do not need to form a perfect central research question yet. Instead, aim to understand the field well enough that narrowing down becomes easier in the next stage.

In Tutorial meeting 1, you practice this process by creating a shared overview (mind map) based on the articles. This page mirrors that activity.

2. Useful sources for orientation

To orient yourself, use sources that are designed to provide structured introductions and overviews. Examples include textbooks, subject encyclopaedias, handbooks, and review articles. These sources help you understand the vocabulary of the field and the main perspectives that are commonly discussed.

In AWS I, you receive a literature set of five articles related to your chosen subject. Use these as your starting point to get a general view and to identify sub areas. Later, you will search for additional sources to match your final direction.

3. Exercise (Tutorial meeting 1 activity)

Part 1. Create a mind map (25 minutes)

Place your topic in the center. Based on the articles you read, add sub areas around it. Add branches for key concepts, terms, theories, methods, variables, or findings that appear important. The aim is to make the topic structure visible, not to create a perfect map.

Part 2. Generate possible focus directions (15 minutes)

Look at your mind map and identify areas you are curious about. You can think in terms of theory versus application, an interesting relationship between variables, an area of application (for example, education, workplace, clinical practice), or a target group (for example, children, adults, a patient group). The goal is to identify directions that could later become a central question.

Part 3. Overview reading and critical evaluation

Searching and selecting literature can be time consuming. To stay focused, keep your topic and emerging direction in mind and avoid including information that has no bearing on your subject. A quick way to check relevance is to read the abstract first. If the abstract looks relevant, continue with the introduction and conclusion or discussion to get the main question, logic, and take away.

A practical set of questions to apply while reading (overview level):

  • What is the central question of the article?
  • Which problem is discussed, and why is it relevant?
  • What are the central terms or concepts?
  • Which theory or theories underlie the research question?
  • What is the conclusion or answer to the central question?
  • What is the basis for the conclusion (own research, analysis of existing research, or both)?
  • What is one critical comment you can make about this article?

A useful reading strategy is to work in rounds. You can start with title and abstract for relevance, then read introduction and discussion for a general impression. If needed, scan method and results (including tables and figures) to understand what was done. Finally, add one critical note.

4. Relevance and the research puzzle

Scientific writing usually begins with a puzzle or problem. A puzzle can appear when theories disagree, when theory does not explain an empirical finding, or when different studies appear to contradict each other.

In this stage, you do not need to resolve the puzzle. It is enough to notice where tension, disagreement, or an open question might exist. In the next stage, you will use this understanding to narrow down and formulate your central research question.

5. Reading articles critically

The goal of critical reading is to question what you read, identify strengths and weaknesses, and judge how an article fits within the broader academic context.

A structured reading strategy

Apply multiple reading rounds.

  1. Round 1: Read the title and abstract and decide whether the article is relevant.
  2. Round 2: Read the introduction and discussion and understand the research question and conclusion.
  3. Round 3: Read the method and results and see how the conclusion was reached.
  4. Round 4: Critically evaluate the article.

Critical mindset

  • What is the main goal of the article?
  • What evidence is presented?
  • Does the evidence support the conclusion?
  • What are the limitations?
  • How does this article fit within the broader literature?

Empirical articles

  • Sample size
  • Study design
  • Operationalisation
  • Reliability and validity
  • Results and statistics
  • Control for confounders
  • Generalisation
  • Scientific context

Review articles

  • Do the arguments support the conclusion?
  • Is the review relevant?
  • Is the review up to date?
  • Is the review balanced?
  • Are the sources varied?
  • Are primary or secondary sources used?

Tip: Your mind map and notes from the questions above are inputs for the next step, where you formulate one central research question.

Using GenAI in AWS I

Optional GenAI support for topic exploration

If you want to use GenAI in this stage, treat it as a coach that helps you clarify your own thinking. Do the exercise first. Then use GenAI to strengthen the way you describe your mind map in words.

A useful twist is to focus on naming two things: the nature of concepts and the relationships between concepts. You do not need perfect labels. Your wording should be logically consistent and grounded in what you actually read.

What GenAI can help you with

  • Ask clarifying questions about what you mean by a concept in your mind map
  • Help you name a concept type in a reasonable way (for example theory, method, sample, outcome, intervention, mechanism)
  • Help you name relationship types (for example influences, predicts, is part of, contrasts with, is measured by, is explained by)
  • Help you rewrite your own descriptions more clearly using synonyms or paraphrases without changing your meaning

What to avoid

  • Do not ask GenAI to generate your full mind map from scratch
  • Do not ask GenAI to finalise your central research question in this stage
  • Do not copy output without checking if it matches your articles and your intended meaning
