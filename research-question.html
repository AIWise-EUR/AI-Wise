Formulating a Central Research Question

In this step, you move from exploring your topic to formulating possible central research questions. Based on the directions you identified earlier, try to formulate two or three candidate questions. The sections below explain what a central research question is and provide criteria to assess whether your question is suitable for a literature review. Once you have a candidate question, discuss it with your tutor or course coordinator to confirm that it fits the assignment and the course expectations.

The content below is based on course materials in Canvas, specifically Appendix III and the PowerPoint slides from Meeting 2.

Appendix III:The Central question

Having a central question will guide your direction of search for literature. Your central question must be specific and clearly formulated and should not cover too much or too little scope. Here is an example of a central question with too much scope: "How do people influence your behaviour?" Answering this question could require the production of several volumes of text. We also do not know which type of behaviour is referred to here, rendering the question somewhat vague. Here is an example of a central question with too little scope: "How do depressive stepfathers influence the playing behaviour of three year olds?" This question is too specific, which is why you will probably have a hard time finding sufficient information on this subject.

A good question should meet the following requirements. Your question should:

  • be clearly formulated;
  • be specific;
  • not contain any vague terminology;
  • not be too wide or too narrow in its scope;
  • be an open question;
  • be a single question.

You might want to start off with a more general question, narrowing down your scope once you have read some articles on the issue.

An example: Let us say you are interested in the subject of addiction. People can become addicted to all kinds of things, from heroine to alcohol, from sex to sweets. So you decide to focus on alcohol addiction and come up with the following question: "What is alcohol addiction?". You will then go looking for literature to give you an overview of the theories and research that has been done on alcohol addiction. And it turns out that there is enough material available out there for you to write an entire book on the subject, rather than just a review. You will find information about the process of addiction, risk groups, social influences, biological predisposition, prevention programmes and cultural variations. You will need to define your subject more specifically. So you decide to choose the area that interests you most and finally come to a specific central question: "What role do social factors play in adolescents becoming addicted to alcohol?".

Types of Questions
  • Comparison: Difference between X and Y?
  • Evaluation: What is the effect of...? How effective is...?
  • Explanation: Why...? Cause of...? Influence of...?
Criteria to assess central question
1. Relevance

Scientific and or Societal relevance. Students should be able to argue why it is interesting to answer the question.

The relevance of the central question

A scientific article aims to contribute to science. As a prerequisite of this, the question discussed in it should have scientific relevance. Scientific writing often begins with a puzzle (problem). Any research is often triggered by an issue which has up to then not been solved in a satisfactory manner. The puzzle could be made up of the following combinations: theory v theory; theory v empirical research; or empirical research v empirical research. In the theory v theory puzzle different theories attempt to explain the same phenomenon. In the theory v empirical research puzzle an empirical phenomenon is discussed for which theory has not yet been able to provide an explanation. In the empirical research v empirical research puzzle research results from different studies are examined for their apparent contradiction.

In order to properly map the relevance of a question, clarity must be attained on the following issues:

  • the problem the question pertains to;
  • the origin of the problem;
  • what makes the problem a problem;
  • why the problem is relevant;
  • why the problem needs tackling.
How relevant to my search question is the literature I have found?

Once you have got a clear idea of which question you would like to discuss in your report, you can then proceed to search for relevant literature. Searching for literature can be tough, time consuming and frustrating. The biggest pitfalls in this regard are losing focus, losing track of the bigger picture and selecting irrelevant information.

To maintain your focus you are advised to stick to your central question as much as you can when in search of relevant literature. When you come across information that has no bearing on your topic, do not include it in your report. One quick way of checking whether an article is relevant is to read its summary. This will always list the research question and the main findings. If that appears interesting, then continue on to reading its introduction and conclusion.

Finding an article of interest may help you redirect your search and gain new search terms to employ in your quest for relevant articles. It may also point you to information you will still need and spur you on to go looking for that. The information you encounter may also lead you to amend your question, as your search may lead you to find that too little or even too much information is on offer. Do not be afraid of tweaking your question, if you feel you need to. It can save you a lot of trouble and work.

2. Clarity

Unambiguous, correct and concise.

3. Specificity (Depth of content)

Good delimitation

The question should not be too superficial, so as to award sufficient depth to the report. Questions formulated too generally tend to generate reports summarising fact upon fact, as there simply is not enough room to discuss the sub topics in any great detail. The question must be specific in order to create sufficient depth of content in the report.

4. Open

Room for alternatives, explanation, in depth discussion.

5. Feasibility
  • Do you have an idea of sub topics you would like to elaborate on?
  • Can the question be answered in 1759 to 2250 words?
  • Assess whether the question can be answered by a short literature report.

Using GenAI in this stage

For this stage, we strongly recommend formulating your central research question without GenAI.

Your central research question should reflect your own interests, perspective, and reasoning about your assigned topic. There is no statistically “best” central question. A question may sound well written, but still be a poor fit for your assignment, your assigned subject, or your own motivation.

If you need support, discuss your ideas with your classmates, tutor, or course coordinator. They are familiar with the expectations of the assignment and can best judge whether your question is suitable, clear, and feasible within the scope of this course.

If you still choose to use GenAI, use it only for process support. For example, you may ask it to help you clarify your wording or to challenge your reasoning. Do not copy a generated research question and treat it as your own. Always verify your question with the course guidelines and your tutor.
