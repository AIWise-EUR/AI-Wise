Section 1 · Prompt

Designing the input

A prompt is a text input given to AI to instruct a task. Research defines prompt engineering as "the skill of articulating a problem, its context, and the constraints of the desired solution to an AI assistant" (Federiakin et al., 2024). A scoping review simplifies the components into four: role assignment, context provision, boundary determination, and result verification.

In this module, a prompt is not a command — it is calibration. It is the act of defining what you want to do, what you expect, what process to follow, and under what conditions. Think of it as designing a space for thought — a virtual workspace where your task takes shape before AI processes it. To write a good prompt, you need to understand GenAI, but you also need to understand yourself: your task, your intention, your current stage, and your boundaries.

"

Learning how to interact with AI is not unlike being someone who's really good at asking questions. Prompting AI is very similar. You can't just randomly ask a bunch of questions. Asking AI to be an assistant to you requires some expertise and artistry of how to prompt it.

— Jensen Huang, CEO of NVIDIA (Cleo Abram, "Huge Conversations", January 2025)

Reinterpreting prompt technique research

When research shows a specific prompting technique improves model performance, it means that type of information was genuinely needed for AI's task processing. As models improve, they fill in this information themselves when the user does not provide it. But the information does not become unnecessary — the decision-maker shifts from user to model.

If you don't set it, AI sets it for you. And because AI's output looks plausible, you miss the fact that there was a choice to be made at all.

Six types of information AI uses to process a task
1
Clear Instruction
Task Specification
Information about what needs to be done. The more you specify the purpose, scope, context, and conditions of a task, the narrower AI's working space becomes.
If you don't set it: AI interprets the task in the most generic way possible. Whether it matches your intent is left to chance.
✗ "Summarize this paper"
✓ "Summarize this paper's main argument and research methodology in two sentences each. This is for an undergraduate psychology class presentation."
Consensus: treated as baseline across all major prompt engineering surveys
2
Role / Persona
Perspective Assignment
Information about what perspective AI should operate from. Setting a role is not a technique to boost AI accuracy — it is the act of deciding what position you want AI to occupy for this task.
If you don't set it: AI operates as a generic "helpful assistant."
✗ "Check my Boolean search string"
✓ "You are a search strategy advisor for academic databases. Review the Boolean search string I wrote. If there are logical errors, don't fix them directly — ask me questions about what went wrong."
Evidence: no measurable accuracy improvement on benchmarks (Zheng et al., 2024 EMNLP; 162 personas, 4 LLM families). Effective as a tone and framing tool.
3
Task Decomposition
Structure of a Task
Information about the structure of a task. When you throw a complex task at AI in one piece, you cannot tell which part of the output went wrong.
If you don't set it: AI decomposes the task on its own, but you cannot know if its decomposition matches your intent.
✗ "Build my literature review outline"
✓ Step 1: "Extract the common themes from the five papers I read" → Step 2: "Map the relationships between those themes" → Step 3: "Suggest possible outline structures based on those relationships"
Evidence: Least-to-Most prompting showed 83.5pp gain on compositional benchmarks (Zhou et al., 2022 ICLR)
4
Few-shot Examples
Desired Output
Information about what the desired output looks like. The act of creating an example forces you to check: do I actually know what I want? Modern models can infer intent without examples, but that means the model interprets on its own.
If you don't set it: AI responds in its most generic form. Without a standard, you cannot judge if it is right.
✓ "Summarize each paper's core claim in one sentence, like this:
Smith (2022): Social media use is positively associated with anxiety in adolescents, mediated by sleep disruption.
Do the same for the remaining three papers."
Evidence: established in Brown et al. (2020, NeurIPS) GPT-3 paper. Few-shot CoT scored highest in Schulhoff et al. benchmarks.
5
Chain-of-Thought
Reasoning Steps
Information about the reasoning process. Asking AI to show its intermediate steps opens a window for you to check its logic.
If you don't set it: You get only the conclusion, with no visibility into how it got there. If it sounds plausible, you just accept it.
✗ "Are the conclusions of these two papers contradictory?"
✓ "Determine whether the conclusions of these two papers contradict each other. First, summarize each paper's core claim. Then compare where they overlap and where they differ. Finally, explain step by step whether the difference is a genuine contradiction or a difference in scope or conditions."
Evidence: Wei et al. (2022, NeurIPS) GSM8K 18% → 58%. However, effects are limited outside math/symbolic reasoning (Sprague et al., 2024).
6
Output Format
Shape of the Output
Information about the shape of the output. The same content delivered as a table, a list, a single sentence, or a set of questions changes how you can use it afterward. Specifying the format is not a convenience — it is designing how the output will integrate into your workflow.
If you don't set it: AI defaults to its standard form (usually long paragraphs).
✗ "Compare these three papers"
✓ "Create a comparison table for these three papers. Columns: author/year, research question, methodology, key findings, limitations. One sentence per cell."
Revisited in Section 3 (Integrate) from the integration strategy perspective.
Proactive GenAI Usage

If you fill in these six components before using AI, that is proactive usage. GenAI is a speed tool. Speed means these decisions are made by the model before you even notice. Proactive GenAI usage means having your own ideas about these components before you let AI work.

My Task
1. Clear Instruction
  • What exactly am I trying to do right now?
  • What is the purpose of this task?
  • What is the scope?
  • Which stage of my project does this task belong to?
2. Role
  • What perspective should AI operate from?
  • What should AI NOT do?
  • What tone and level of response do I need?
3. Task Decomposition
  • Can I break this task into smaller units?
  • Am I trying to ask for too much at once?
  • What order should the sub-tasks follow?
4. Few-shot
  • Do I know what the desired output looks like?
  • Can I create one example?
  • If I can't make an example, do I actually know what I want?
5. Chain-of-Thought
  • What reasoning process does this task require?
  • What steps should AI go through?
  • Do I need to see the intermediate process?
6. Output Format
  • What format do I need to actually use it?
  • Table? List? Single sentence? Questions?
  • Is this format easy to integrate into my workflow?
Quick prompt skeleton
[Role] You are a _______ who _______. [Task] I need you to _______. [Context] I am currently working on _______. This task is part of _______. [Constraints] Do not _______. Stay within _______. [Reasoning] Approach this by first _______, then _______. [Examples] Here is an example of what I want: _______ [Format] Respond in the form of a _______.
Section 2 · Critique

Output is not an answer — it is a candidate

What AI gives you is not a final answer but a candidate: candidate structure, candidate wording, candidate comparison, candidate interpretation. Output is material to review, not material to adopt.

This connects directly to what you learned in C1. GenAI output is statistically likely, not factually verified. It is conditioned on prior tokens, not on reasoning. It is bounded by training data. It reflects a distribution, not authorship. Without your domain knowledge, critique is impossible (Zhu & Duan, 2025).

Four steps: Read → Question → Verify → Decide
1
Read
Identify what came out
  • Is this a response to what I requested?
  • What structure did AI use? (list, paragraphs, table, etc.)
  • What overall claim or direction is it taking?
Example
Student asked "Extract the common themes from these three papers." AI returned three paragraphs. Observation: AI identified three themes, assigned papers to each, and wrote in paragraph form.
2
Question
Generate questions before judging
  • Is this content factually verifiable?
  • Is anything missing?
  • Are any parts overly general or vague?
  • Does the output include claims I can't verify?
  • Did the output stay within my requested scope?
Example
Are these three themes actually from the papers, or did AI generalize? Is the third theme too broad? Paper B is assigned to two themes — is that accurate? I only asked for common themes, but AI added "implications" — did it exceed my request scope?
3
Verify
Judge against criteria
  • If facts, citations, or numbers are included — can the source be verified?
  • Does the output match the scope of my request?
  • Can I explain this content myself?
  • Does this output reflect my intent and context?
Example
Checking Paper A: AI's first theme is correct but was a secondary point, not the core finding. The third theme uses phrasing that doesn't appear in any original paper — AI generalized. The "implications" section covers a stage I haven't reached yet.
4
Decide
Select, modify, or discard
  • Which parts can I use and which should I discard?
  • Which parts need modification?
  • Is a re-prompt necessary?
Example
Themes 1 and 2 are usable as starting points. Theme 3 is discarded. The implications section is deleted. Theme 2's paper assignment needs verification — may re-prompt after checking the original.
Section 3 · Integrate

How AI output enters your work

Integration does not start after you receive the output — it starts at the prompting stage. The moment you decide what format to receive the output in, you have already determined how safe and controllable the integration will be.

"Compare these papers" produces paragraphs that easily bleed into your writing. "Make a comparison table" keeps the output separate from your text, verifiable cell by cell. Choosing the output format is not a convenience — it is a safety mechanism for integration.

Requested FormatIntegration Characteristics
Comparison tableVerifiable and extractable cell by cell. Does not blend into your prose.
Question listServes as a thinking prompt. No copy-paste risk. You have to write the answers.
Single sentence summaryA single claim — easy to accept or reject clearly.
Keyword / term listEasy to selectively adopt. You write the sentences.
Bullet point outlineUse the structure as reference; fill in the content yourself.
Full paragraphHighest risk of blending into your writing. Use with caution.
Adopt / Modify / Discard
1
Segment
Break the output into units that can be judged independently. Rows for tables, items for lists, claims for paragraphs.
2
Tag
Assign a tag to each unit: Adopt accurate, matches my intent, ready to use — Modify direction is right, needs adjustment — Discard inaccurate, outside scope, or I cannot verify it.
3
Execute
Adopt → place in your work; confirm source and context. Modify → define what to change; return to original source or re-prompt. Discard → delete; record why in one line.
4
Review
After integration, look at the whole: Does this reflect my thinking? Has AI's language remained unchanged in my text? Can I explain and defend every part?
Worked example — Common theme extraction

Student asked AI to extract common themes from three papers. AI presented three themes:

Theme 1: "Sleep disruption as a mediator between screen time and adolescent anxiety"
Adopt
Matches the original papers. Usable as an outline starting point. Placed as starting point for first outline section.
Theme 2: "The role of parental monitoring in mitigating digital harm"
Modify
Direction is correct but AI generalized "social media exposure" into "digital harm." Corrected to "social media exposure." Original paper re-checked.
Theme 3: "Broader implications for public health policy"
Discard
I asked for common themes, not implications. This topic does not appear in the papers. AI fabricated it. Deleted. Record: "AI exceeded request scope by adding implications."
Human Accountability Checklist
  • Are the core ideas and judgments in this work my own?
  • Have I verified all AI-generated content that I adopted?
  • Can I explain and defend every part of this work?
  • Can I transparently disclose how AI was used?
Based on the Author Checklist from Cheng, Calhoun, & Reedy (2025). Advances in Simulation, 10, 22.
Closing · Template

Building your prompt

AI processes structured input more accurately than prose paragraphs. Formatting changes alone can shift accuracy by up to 76 percentage points (Sclar et al., ICLR 2024). Two common structuring formats are Markdown and XML.

Markdown
# Role You are a literature analysis assistant. # Task Identify common themes across three papers. # Context I am writing a literature review for AWS. # Constraints - Do not summarize each paper individually - Stay at theme identification only # Output Format Numbered list, one sentence per theme.
XML
<role> You are a literature analysis assistant. </role> <task> Identify common themes across three papers. </task> <context> I am writing a literature review for AWS. </context> <constraints> Do not summarize each paper individually. Stay at theme identification only. </constraints> <output_format> Numbered list, one sentence per theme. </output_format>

Markdown uses # for headings and - for lists — intuitive and easy for humans to read. XML uses explicit opening and closing tags, which models parse more precisely. XML is the only delimiter format recommended by all three major providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google), making it advantageous for complex prompts.

Prompt template (XML)
<role> [What perspective should AI operate from?] </role> <task> [What specifically needs to be done? Include purpose and context] </task> <context> [The situation this task sits in. Current stage, background, materials] </context> <constraints> [What should AI NOT do? Scope limits. Conditions to respect] </constraints> <steps> [The reasoning or work steps AI should follow] 1. ... 2. ... 3. ... </steps> <examples> [Examples of the desired output. 1–3] </examples> <output_format> [Shape, length, and structure of the output] </output_format>
Prompt length guide
Task ComplexityRecommended Length
Simple50–100 words (summary, factual question)
Moderate150–300 words (analysis, comparison, structured output)
Complex300–500 words (multi-step reasoning, detailed generation)
Beyond 500 wordsSplit into multiple turns
Writing principles
Use imperative verbs
Start instructions with action words."Summarize", "Compare", "Identify"
Skip politeness tokens
"Please", "thank you", and "if you don't mind" are unnecessary and can reduce performance (Dobariya & Kumar, 2025).
Use affirmative instructions
Affirmative instructions outperform negations."Use plain language" > "Don't use jargon"
Place key instructions at the edges
Put the most important instructions at the very beginning or very end of your prompt, countering the "Lost in the Middle" effect (Liu et al., 2024).

This template is a general-purpose framework. The activity preset prompts in this module are instances of this template with each field pre-filled for a specific learning activity.

Full worked example — Common theme extraction from three papers
<role> You are an academic literature analysis assistant for a first-year psychology student. </role> <task> Identify the common themes across the following three journal articles. Focus on overlapping research topics, not methodological similarities. </task> <context> I am writing a literature review for my Academic Writing Skills course. I have read all three papers. I am currently at the stage of organizing my sources before drafting an outline. </context> <constraints> Do not summarize each paper individually. Do not suggest an outline or structure. Stay at the level of theme identification only. </constraints> <steps> 1. Read the three abstracts I provide below. 2. Identify recurring topics or constructs. 3. Group them into distinct themes. </steps> <examples> Example theme format: "Sleep disruption as a mediator between screen time and adolescent anxiety (Paper A, Paper C)" </examples> <output_format> A numbered list of 3–5 common themes. Each theme in one sentence, with the relevant paper(s) noted in parentheses. </output_format>
