C2 · Core Block

What is AI for us?

Knowing what GenAI is made of is a starting point. The next question is what it can and cannot do compared to you, and how to work with it effectively.

Section 1

Why they differ

Before comparing what humans and GenAI can do, it is necessary to understand why they differ in the first place. The differences are not superficial. They are structural, rooted in how each system receives and processes information.

Part A: Input channel asymmetry

Think about the last time you saw an elephant. Not in a photo, but in real life. You didn't just see it. You smelled it. You heard it breathe. You felt the ground vibrate slightly under its weight. All of that entered your brain at once, and all of it shaped what "elephant" means to you.

Now think about what happens when a language model encounters the word "elephant." It receives a token. A numerical code that represents the word. That's it. No smell, no sound, no sense of scale. Just a number in a sequence of numbers.

This is the most basic difference between human cognition and GenAI: the input is not the same.

Human

Five senses, all at once. Smell, sound, scale, proximity, integrated into one layered experience. Your brain does not process these separately and stitch them together later. They arrive as a single, unified representation of the world.

GenAI

A token. A numerical code that stands for a word. By the time any input reaches the model, it has already been compressed and stripped down to symbolic structure. The model operates entirely at the level of abstracted language, with no connection to the sensory world the language describes.

✓
Nonsensory meaning: recovered at human level
Abstract, relational knowledge. Things like "elephants are mammals," "they live in herds," "they are associated with memory." These are patterns that exist fully in language itself, and LLMs pick them up about as well as humans do.
~
Sensory features: partially recovered
What something looks, sounds, or feels like. Multimodal models that process images alongside text do better here, especially for vision. But "partially" is the key word: the gap narrows for what can be photographed, not for what can only be experienced.
✗
Motor and bodily features: not recovered
What it feels like to stand next to something enormous. The weight of an object in your hand. The muscle memory of a familiar movement. These dimensions of meaning are grounded in having a body, and no amount of text training has bridged this gap. There is no current pathway by which it would.
Part B: Distance from reality

The information GenAI learns from is not reality. It is a filtered, compressed, and unevenly sampled version of what people have written online.

Think of it like a chain. At each link, something gets lost.

Reality
Everything that happens in the world.
What gets recorded
Only the fraction that someone decides to write down.
What gets published online
Only the fraction of that which makes it onto the internet.
What gets indexed
Only what search engines and web crawlers can find and collect.
What gets selected for training data
Only what dataset builders choose to include.
What the model learns
The statistical average of everything above.

At every step, noise enters and fidelity decreases. The result is a model trained on the statistical average of online text, which is itself a narrow and unrepresentative sample of human experience.

To give you a sense of scale: GPT 3 was trained on hundreds of billions of words. About 82% of those words came from a single source, a massive collection of web pages scraped automatically from the internet. Research has shown that this collection systematically overrepresents certain languages, cultures, and demographics. Some researchers argue that this is not simply a technical problem with a straightforward fix, but something more deeply tied to how large language models work: they predict text based on what they have seen, so the patterns in the training data inevitably shape the patterns in the output.

What this means for output
In creativity tasks, GenAI tends to raise the floor. It produces consistently competent results. But the ceiling is lower: the highest quality human outputs exceed what GenAI produces, and attempts to boost LLM creativity through prompt engineering have yielded mixed to negative results.
Part C: Intentional information loss

When you ask GenAI something, you type a prompt. But think about everything that shaped your question before you started typing. Your background knowledge. Your assumptions about what matters. Your emotional investment in the topic. The context you are working in. The criteria you have in your head but never spelled out.

Most of that never makes it into the prompt. It can't. You would need to write pages just to capture what you already know without thinking about it.

GenAI has access only to what is explicitly provided. What is not typed does not exist for the model. This creates a structural gap between what you mean and what the model receives.

Here is what that looks like in practice.

Example 1 of 6 Connecting studies in a literature review
What you are actually thinking

I need to find a way to connect these two studies for my literature review. One is about social media and self esteem, the other is about screen time and sleep. My professor said the transition between paragraphs matters a lot. I tried linking them through "adolescent wellbeing" but it felt too vague. I want something more specific but I am not sure what that would look like.

What you type

[uploads two PDFs]
"How do I connect these two studies in my literature review?"

How the model actually processes this

Full text of both papers is processed. Themes, findings, methodologies, theoretical frameworks all extracted. The most statistically likely "helpful" response pattern for "connect these two studies" is activated. → Generate a comprehensive answer covering multiple possible connection points: shared constructs, overlapping populations, complementary findings, theoretical alignment. Present them as options. Sound confident and thorough.

Example 2 of 6 Creating practice questions
What you are actually thinking

My exam is next week and I want to test myself on chapter 4. The professor always writes tricky MCQs where two options look almost the same and you have to know the exact definition to tell them apart. I need questions that are at that level, not the easy ones where you can guess by elimination.

What you type

[pastes chapter 4 text]
"Make me some MCQs on chapter 4."

How the model actually processes this

Full chapter text is processed. Key concepts, definitions, and relationships identified. "MCQ" triggers a default format: one correct answer, three distractors, medium difficulty, recall focused. → Generate questions that cover the chapter evenly, make distractors plausible but clearly distinguishable from the correct answer.

Example 3 of 6 Summarizing a paper
What you are actually thinking

I read this paper but I am not sure I understood the method section. I think they used a between subjects design but the way they describe the groups is confusing. I do not need a full summary, I just need someone to explain the design so I can figure out whether their conclusion actually follows from it.

What you type

[uploads PDF]
"Can you summarize this paper?"

How the model actually processes this

Full paper is processed. All sections weighted. "Summarize" triggers a default structure: background, aim, method, key findings, conclusion. Roughly equal coverage across sections. → Produce something that reads like a complete, balanced overview. Sound clear and organized.

Example 4 of 6 Understanding a concept
What you are actually thinking

The professor used the term "ecological validity" in the lecture today and I kind of got it from context, but when I tried to explain it to my friend I couldn't. I think it has something to do with how realistic a study is, but I am not sure if it is about the setting, the task, or both. And I am confused about how it is different from external validity because they sound like they overlap.

What you type

"What is ecological validity?"

How the model actually processes this

A definition request. "Ecological validity" is a well represented concept in the training data. → Generate a clear, textbook style definition. Add an example. Possibly mention related terms. Present a self contained explanation, covering the concept broadly rather than targeting any specific point of confusion.

Example 5 of 6 Asking for feedback on your writing
What you are actually thinking

I spent two hours on this introduction and I think the argument flows okay, but the last sentence feels weak. My tutor told me last time that I tend to state things too broadly without committing to a clear position. I want to know if I am doing that again here, specifically in the last two sentences.

What you type

[pastes paragraph]
"What do you think of this? Any feedback?"

How the model actually processes this

A paragraph is provided. Feedback requested with no specific focus. → Scan for grammar, clarity, structure, and argument strength across the entire paragraph. Generate a balanced set of comments covering multiple aspects. Highlight positives first, then suggest improvements. Distribute attention evenly across the whole text.

Example 6 of 6 Writing a motivation letter
What you are actually thinking

I am applying for this internship at a research lab and I really want it because the supervisor published a paper I referenced in my thesis. I have some relevant experience from my stats course and a small project I did with a classmate, but I am not sure if that counts. The deadline is tomorrow and I saw that they value "independent thinkers" but I do not want to sound arrogant.

What you type

"Help me write a motivation letter for a research internship."

How the model actually processes this

A motivation letter for a research internship. No specific lab, no field, no applicant background, no deadline pressure, no tone preference. → Generate a polished, general purpose motivation letter template. Use broadly applicable language about "passion for research" and "eagerness to learn." Structure it in a standard format. Sound professional and enthusiastic.

In every one of these examples, the model did something reasonable with what it received. The problem is not that it did a bad job. The problem is that most of what mattered to you never reached the model in the first place.

This gap does not disappear as you get better at prompting. You can narrow it, but you cannot close it.

Research supports this. Studies have found that language models generate responses with significantly less conversational grounding than humans. Rather than actively checking whether they understood you correctly, they tend to assume that common ground already exists. And here is an interesting complication: the fine tuning that makes models more helpful and polished actually makes this worse. The more "assistant like" the model becomes, the less likely it is to pause and verify what you actually meant.

Across longer conversations, this adds up. Even for the most advanced models available today, average performance on generation tasks drops by about 39% in multi turn conversations compared to fully specified single turn instructions.

Section 2

Human vs GenAI: a functional comparison

Section 1 established why humans and GenAI differ. The input is different, the training data is distant from reality, and most of what you mean never reaches the model. But what do these structural differences actually look like when the two systems perform specific tasks?

This section compares ten cognitive functions, grouped into three categories based on where the advantage lies.

H
Human leads
Perception
Humans receive the world through five senses at once, integrated into one experience. GenAI receives tokens. The gap is largest for bodily, physical experience, and partially closed for vision in multimodal models.
Emotional processing
Humans experience emotion as a genuine internal state that shapes thinking, motivation, and behavior. GenAI recognizes and produces emotionally appropriate language, but has no internal feeling behind it.
Metacognition
Humans know what they know and what they don't. They monitor their own confidence and adjust. GenAI produces confidence estimates through probability distributions, but has no persistent self check. This is part of why it can say something false without hesitating.
Social cognition
Humans develop social understanding through real relationships, emotional resonance, and years of interaction. GenAI can pass structured social reasoning tests at high levels, but without the developmental grounding and relational stakes behind it.
↔
Both capable, depends on the task
Short term storage
Human working memory holds roughly four to seven chunks and actively manipulates them. GenAI context windows hold thousands of tokens passively. Studies show comparable limits under active maintenance conditions, but humans manipulate held information more flexibly.
Reasoning
Humans support causal, counterfactual, and analogical reasoning and adapt flexibly to new situations. GenAI outperforms humans on some structured reasoning tasks, but fails on tasks that require distinguishing observation from intervention, or deliberately exploring uncertain options.
Language processing
Humans integrate grammar with pragmatics, social intent, and contextual meaning. GenAI achieves strong grammatical competence but functional competence (using language appropriately in context) is uneven. The key insight: language ability and thinking ability are separate systems, and GenAI has acquired one without fully having the other.
Creativity
Humans produce highly variable creative output, with the best human work exceeding anything GenAI produces. GenAI raises the floor by producing consistently competent results, but the upper tail is smaller. Attempts to boost GenAI creativity through prompt engineering have yielded mixed to negative results.
G
GenAI leads on scale
Pattern recognition
GenAI detects statistical regularities across billions of examples with consistency and speed beyond human capacity. On structured analogy tasks, it has matched or surpassed human performance, though results vary significantly across model versions and task types.
Long term storage
GenAI encodes patterns from its entire training corpus into billions of parameters, far exceeding human memory in raw volume. But this knowledge is frozen at the training cutoff and cannot be updated through conversation. Human memory holds less, but keeps learning from lived experience.

Section 3

A framework for collaboration

"The beginning is always yours"

Every intellectual task begins with something that cannot be generated. Not a prompt, not a structure, not a draft. It begins with a felt sense that something matters. A question that has not been asked yet. A problem noticed but not named. An intention that exists before any words have been found for it.

GenAI cannot produce this. Not because it lacks capability in any technical sense, but because it has no stake in the world. It does not feel the gap between what is and what could be. It responds to what is given, but it cannot generate the impulse to begin.

This is the ignition point. You bring a direction, however vague. A sense of what matters. The willingness to commit to a question before knowing how to answer it. None of this can be prompted into existence. It arrives with you, or it does not arrive at all.

"GenAI's role: Operationalization"

Once you have that starting point, the next step is still yours. You sketch out an idea. A rough structure, a basic concept, a set of notes, a first attempt at putting your intention into words. It does not need to be polished. It does not need to be complete. But it needs to exist. Something on paper that reflects what you are trying to do.

This is where GenAI becomes useful. You hand it your rough draft, your notes, your half formed structure, and GenAI turns it into something more developed. It fills in gaps, suggests organization, produces a version that is concrete enough to react to. You judge that output, redirect, and GenAI produces again. Within minutes, work that might have taken hours to develop on your own has been shaped into a real draft.

This is what GenAI does best in collaboration: it operationalizes. Not from nothing, but from something you have already started. It takes your rough thinking and makes it tangible, fast. And the process accelerates. Each output gives you more to react to, which gives GenAI more to work with, which produces richer output.

GenAI is not thinking instead of you. It is making your thinking visible fast enough to work with.

"How the loop works"

You start with an intention. GenAI operationalizes it into something concrete. You judge the output: is this what I meant? Is this useful? What is missing? You feed that judgment back, and GenAI produces again. Each cycle sharpens the work.

In this loop, you are doing something that GenAI cannot do for itself. You are supplying the intent, the quality criteria, the sense of direction. GenAI is doing something you cannot do as quickly on your own. It is generating structured output at a speed that lets you focus entirely on evaluation and steering.

"When momentum drops"

But this loop does not run forever. At some point, you will notice the momentum dropping. The signs are specific:

The model keeps returning to assumptions it fixed early in the conversation, even though your requirements have shifted across turns.

The model delivers a "complete" answer without asking any clarifying questions.

The outputs are internally consistent, but you sense they might be wrong in ways the model cannot detect on its own.

These are not failures. They are the natural limits of what a single Human+GenAI session can produce. The work has been operationalized, but it has not yet been tested against reality.

"The team's role: Expansion"

This is where the team comes in. When the GenAI loop stalls, you bring the work to people. Your classmates, your tutor, your project group. The team brings what GenAI structurally cannot: shared institutional context, social judgment, real world experience with the problem, and perspectives shaped by lives you have not lived.

The team does not just polish what you already have. It expands the scope. New ideas you had not considered. Constraints you did not know existed. Feedback that reshapes the direction of the work entirely. After this interaction, the work is no longer just yours and GenAI's. It belongs to a larger reality.

"How this loop is different"

The team loop works differently from the GenAI loop. You present the work. The team gives feedback. You integrate that feedback, and in the process, new questions emerge, new angles open up, new things need to be considered. The scope does not just refine. It grows.

Where GenAI operationalizes what you already had in mind, the team introduces what you did not have in mind. This is the difference between acceleration and expansion.

"Back to GenAI: a new cycle begins"

After the team interaction, you do not return to where you left off. You return to GenAI with new material. The team's feedback, revised constraints, new directions. And you initiate a new operationalization cycle. The same pattern repeats: rapid progress, visible output, accelerating momentum. But this time the starting point is higher. The work has already been tested against reality, and the new cycle builds on that foundation.

"The full structure"

The full framework is a series of these cycles, connected by transitions between partners.

GenAI session → Team session → GenAI session → Team session.

Each GenAI session operationalizes rapidly. Each team session expands the scope. Each transition carries the gains of the previous cycle into the next one. The work never loses what it has gained. It only builds.

"The ending is also yours"

Just as the beginning cannot be generated, the ending cannot be delegated. By the time the work reaches its final form, it carries weight. It will be read, evaluated, acted upon, or built upon by others. It has consequences. And consequences require someone who stands behind the work.

GenAI has no identity, no reputation, no continuity across interactions. It cannot be held responsible. When the work is submitted, your name is attached. Your judgment is what the work represents.

At the end, you judge the final output against your original intent. You decide what stays and what goes. You take responsibility for every claim it makes. This is not a formality. It is the point where the work becomes yours.

"Why this matters"

GenAI operates in the middle. It is extraordinarily capable in that space. It can accelerate, externalize, structure, and refine. But it cannot generate the reason to begin, and it cannot carry the responsibility of finishing.

The beginning and the end are where meaning enters and exits the process. At the start, you bring intent. At the end, you bring accountability. These belong to you, not by convention, but by the nature of what they are.

GenAI accelerates. The team expands. You steer.

In the context of this course, a slowdown is not a failure. It is a signal to switch partners.
