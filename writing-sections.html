Writing Sections of the Literature Review

A literature report consists of a title, abstract, introduction, main body, discussion and reference list. In addition, there are a number of general aspects that you should note. This concerns the general structure, the coherence, the depth of content, the writing style and the references in the text. Below you will find a brief explanation of the various elements.

The content below is based on course materials in Canvas, Annex VII on components of the report

Title

The title should be concise and reflect what the literature report is about. The title must show the main result or the main conclusion of the report - this way the reader will immediately know what the most important information is. Although accuracy is more important than a catchy title, a catchy title does increase the likelihood that the literature report will be read.

Abstract

An abstract should, using no more than 250 words, restate the essence of your report, but in such a way that someone who has not read it would understand it. Briefly state the aim of the report and what its principle findings are. The abstract should be one paragraph and should not contain any references, abbreviations, unknown terms or a table of contents. Make sure that the abstract contains the central question, what sort of research your article is based upon (literature review), what the scope is (the domain to which the question applies) and what the most important findings and conclusions are. An abstract is NOT a summation of what is to be discussed, it just gives the principle findings.

Introduction

The introduction introduces your report and presents your central question. It attempts to get the reader interested and persuade him to continue reading. The introduction starts out quite broadly, narrowing the issue down in a step-by-step fashion, to end up at your specific question. If needed, you can elect to explain some of the important terminology contained in your report and also describe some of the research already conducted. The introduction should also explain and justify why you are going to discuss the issue you are about to discuss. It is important to point out the problem area your question pertains to and argue why it is interesting and relevant to answer this particular question. You might wish to consider its scientific and societal relevance. You may conclude your introduction by giving a brief description of the article's structure. This part of the introduction sees you explain to the reader how the rest of your report will look.

Once the reader has finished reading your introduction he should have a clear idea of what you will be evaluating (the central question), why you will be evaluating it (problem area and relevance), and how you will evaluate it (brief description of the article's structure).

Main Body

The main body of your report should offer a comprehensive overview of the literature found. Limit yourself to the information relevant to your central question and show the reader why this information is relevant. Beware to only select the information that is relevant for your own report. If you have read, for example, an article presenting a description of several experiments of which only the first one presented has a bearing on your report, simply stick to this experiment. There will be no need to include the other experiments in this case.

The main body should by no means simply be a summary of the results of various studies conducted. It should be a coherent text in which the various sources used are integrated with one another. So, make sure that the information in the main body is coherent and logical; you can do this by creating links between the various sources and by detailing any common threads, difference, contrasts and shortcomings. By using signal words (words like moreover, firstly/secondly, one the one hand/ on the other hand, since, in brief), interim summaries and introductions to your paragraphs, you will ensure that the links between various sets of research/ symptoms/ statements, etc. are clear to the reader.

Please note: Do not call the main body a 'main body', but divide your story up into separate sections and give each section a fitting title.

Discussion

This component should include your final conclusion, i.e.: the answer to the central question in the report. This should be followed by a comprehensive, critical discussion of the findings. In this regard it's important to highlight any connections, contradictions, missing information and inconsistencies. In your critical evaluation you might choose to discuss the following issues: What still remains unclear from the literature? What are the suggestions for follow-up research? What are the implications of your findings for science and practice? A good conclusion not only provides an answer to the central question, but also places the implications of this in a wider context, providing the reader with food for thought.

A common misconception among students is that the discussion section may only refer to research cited elsewhere in the report. Of course, the discussion isn't the correct platform for discussing new sources that contribute to answering the central question, as these should have naturally been covered in the main body of the text. But what you are allowed to introduce, in fact, what we'd like you to do, is to cite sources that support a critique or counter argument to your conclusion.

General Structure

A scientific text must be structured in a clear manner. The information should be arranged in a logical order and ideas and arguments should naturally follow each other. You have more or less arranged the general structure of the report by drawing up your draft structure. Now it's time to structure the text on a more specific level by devoting attention to the construction of your sections and paragraphs.

A section is a part of your text devoted to a central issue or idea. Usually, the introduction to an article will be a stand-alone section. This goes for the discussion, too. The main body, on the other hand, should actually be built up of several sections (headers). In general a section will be made up of several paragraphs. A section will begin with an introduction to the topic area or the sub-question featured and conclude with a conclusion and a transition to the next section.

Paragraphs discuss a single subject, line of thought or sub-question and should be connected to the preceding and following paragraphs. In most cases a paragraph will begin with its central issue. This is then followed by and elaboration on or explanation of that issue, and a transitional sentence taking you to the next issue - and paragraph. Paragraphs hence always contain more than one sentence.

Coherence

In the interests of readability, the different components of your report, i.e. paragraphs, sentences, sections, should form a logical coherence. This will allow the reader to easily navigate through the text. Textual coherence is enhanced by the use of structural indicators, i.e. signal words, introductory and transitional sentences and summarising sentences. Structural indicators are words or sentences that serve to highlight the connections between the various textual components. Structural indicators join these elements by explaining their relation to each other and will hence help the reader identify the storyline.

Depth of Content

Creating depth of content isn't done by summarising information, but should exceed that level by adding the author's perspective or opinion to what is being presented. Your report should then serve to clarify your position and attempt to convince the reader of its validity. Logic and structured use of your arguments is, of course, vital to this. The information you've sourced elsewhere should be used to support your story.

Depth of content is achieved by incorporating sources into the text and by also including a critical appreciation of the information you're discussing. Arguments or positions should be properly substantiated by research and/or theory. When presenting these, always include an explanation as to why they support your argument or position.

Please note: You don't necessarily have to reserve any criticism for the discussion section, especially when it comes to detailed connotations. In that case you are better off presenting these in the main body, including them immediately following the description of its related content.

Writing Style

A scientific article should be written in scientific style. A scientific style of writing may be characterised as follows: objective, carefully formulated, clear, concise and concrete.

Please note: Make sure that the text is written in proper English and that it does not contain any spelling and grammar mistakes. Use the Word spell check facility, but don't rely solely on that. Make sure you go over it yourself!

References

A literature report is based on existing literature. In your report you will often find yourself required to refer to other people's ideas, theories and research results. The inclusion of references is important for a number of reasons:

  • transparency: shows where the information comes from and makes it possible to verify the quality of the information
  • modesty: paying respect to the author; not doing so would amount to plagiarism

When referring, you should apply the APA rules to the letter, i.e. each character should be in its correct place. That's why it's important to keep an overview of the APA guidelines in mind when you are inserting references into a text (available from PsyWeb). This is a good way to avoid making unnecessary errors. Also, make sure that all references in the text are listed in the reference list, and that all references in the reference list are included in the text.

Fraud and Plagiarism

As mentioned above, it is important to indicate which ideas belong to other people, by referencing or citing these. If you don't, you would in fact be committing plagiarism. For more information about plagiarism, see http://www.eur.nl/plagiaat.

What is plagiarism?

It is important to understand that using someone else's text without referencing it is plagiarism (a form of fraud). Even copying a sentence from the internet is considered to be plagiarism, just as it would be if you used a sentence written by one of your fellow students. Another form of fraud is allowing your article to be (partly) written by another person. So make sure that you, and you alone, write your literature review yourself. The consequences of plagiarism can be found in the Teaching and Examination Regulations, article 8, part 8 c. The article reads as follows:

'Plagiarism'. Plagiarism is the copying of papers, theses and other written work by third parties. Punishment for this offence will, in the first instance, consist of a non-review of papers (and therefore no mark being awarded) and, secondly, lead to an instruction that a new paper must be submitted that will be subjected to much stricter requirements. Depending on the degree of the offence, students may also be excluded from exams for a period of at least one half year and a maximum of one whole year, as a fitting punishment. It should also be noted that this is also a punishable offence under copyright law. The Exam Commission has decided that as of now they will, where possible, allow papers and theses to be submitted electronically. These papers will then be scanned for plagiarism, using the most up-to-date software available.

How do we check for plagiarism?

All reports will be scanned by a programme that recognises overlaps in texts. This includes recognition of sentences that are formulated in a slightly different way, but which use the same words. If papers are found to have a high level of plagiarism, your paper will be carefully checked by the practical coordinator and the Examination Board and checked for references and citations.

Tips for avoiding plagiarism:

  • Keep track of the literature you've used.
  • When writing a paper, immediately note down which source you have used for that piece. If you make notes for use later on in your paper, ensure that you do them in your own words and that you do not include them verbatim. Verbatim transposing and transposing texts adding only minor tweaks is also considered plagiarism.
  • If you are unsure about how to make a reference, check the APA rules. If you are still struggling, then make your references as you think best. When it comes to your review you can expect feedback on your referencing skills, so that you know if you are getting it right or not. Never leave out any references!
  • Avoid quotes and put things into your own words as much as you can. If you do decide to quote directly from a text (which means a word-for-word transfer of, e.g., a definition), always use quotation marks and a reference with page number (see APA rules.)

Adjusting Your Sentences to Match Your Intention

This activity helps you polish your sentences so that they accurately reflect your intended meaning and the reading experience you want to create.

It is divided into two parts. First, Grammar Check focuses on identifying and correcting clear grammatical issues at the sentence level. Second, Terminology Options allows you to explore alternative word choices to better match your intention, tone, and level of precision.

The focus is on working at a small scale. You revise one or two sentences at a time, then place them back into your paragraph and evaluate how they fit. Coherence is not handled automatically; you are responsible for checking whether your revisions maintain the overall flow and consistency of your writing.

Rather than rewriting your work, this activity supports you by providing possible options. You decide which changes to accept and how they integrate into your text.
