Searching for and Selecting Scientific Literature

In this step, you begin searching for scientific literature related to your central research question. You will learn how to search for scientific articles using Scopus and how to identify relevant studies for your topic. The goal is to develop a search profile and create an initial list of relevant articles that can be used for your literature review.

1. Creating a search profile

A search profile consists of search terms, which you use to search in databases like Scopus, and the relationships between these terms. You combine search terms with Boolean operators: AND or OR.

Use OR between terms that refer to the same part of your research question: in the example below "eating disorders" OR "anorexia nervosa" OR "binge eating disorder".

Use AND between search terms to find publications which contain a combination of these search terms. In the example the terms that refer to eating disorders and the terms that refer to family relations are combined with AND.

Example of a first search profile

Research question: What role do family relations play in eating disorders?
[This topic can be further limited to the onset of eating disorders or the treatment of eating disorders or specific family relations.]

2. Scopus

Scopus is an abstract and citation database of peer-reviewed literature: scientific journals, books and conference proceedings. It provides an overview of the world's research output in the fields of science, technology, medicine, social sciences, and arts and humanities.

To access Scopus, go to https://libguides.eur.nl/scopus. This link will give you access to Scopus also when you're not on campus or not using EduVPN.

Functionalities in Scopus

  • Question mark ?
    Replaces 1 character in a word
    Example: Wom?n will return women & woman
  • Asterisk *
    Replaces 0 or more characters in a word
    Example: Gam* will return game, gaming, gambling, gamma etc.
  • "double quotation marks"
    The search terms within the quotation marks must appear adjacent to each other in the search results.
    Approximate phrase spelling variants are included. This means you can use question marks & asterisks while using double quotation marks.
  • {between braces}
    The search terms appear in exactly this way in the search results.
    Exact phrase, including any stop words, spaces, and punctuation.

It's possible to save searches and lists of documents in Scopus with an Elsevier/Scopus account. To create an account, click Create account at the top of the page and fill in the form. To save a search, click Save search next to the query box at the top of the search results. You can also use the Search History: click More behind the query and then Save this search.

TIP: when you return to Scopus later, sign in before you start searching. Sometimes after signing in, Scopus goes back to the default and deletes your searches.

3. Searching Scopus - in general

A topic search in Scopus

Select Documents, on the Document search page of Scopus (this is the default setting).

Perform a separate search for each element in your research question. This will make it easier to adjust your search later. Use your search profile.

You can add search boxes by clicking + Add search field below the search box. Search terms belonging to the same element are combined with OR. You can select this operator in the dropdown menu between the search bars. You can also enter all search terms in one search box, with OR between them. By default, Scopus searches in the fields 'Article Title, Abstract, Keywords'. You can change this, but for now, it's a good start.

Click the Search button when you have entered all search terms that belong to one element of your research question.

Repeat this for the remaining elements of your question: Go back to Search (the easiest way is to click Search at the top of the screen) or use the search bar(s) at the top of the search results. When necessary, empty the search bars (or click Reset) and repeat.

You can now combine these separate searches in the Search History at the bottom of the Search page. Mark the queries and click Combine queries. In the Combine queries box, you can adjust the Boolean operator (if needed; AND is the default). Searches belonging to different elements of the research question are combined with AND.

Click Show results to perform the search.

The search results in Scopus

The search results page consists of two parts:

  • In the main panel you see the results of your search; for each result you see the title of the article, the author(s), year of publication, the title of the journal in which the article is published and the number of citations the document received from other documents in Scopus.
  • The left-hand column presents options you can select to further refine your search, such as by year of publication, author, subject area, document type, language, etc. Mark the options, then choose Limit to (keep in the results) or Exclude (delete from the results). Please note: the Subject Areas are assigned to journals, not to individual articles.

About the search results:

  • The most recent articles are on top of the list.
  • You can change the order of results with the option in the right upper corner above the results - for example sort by the number of citations (cited by) or by the name of the first author.
  • When you sort by relevance, Scopus will look, among other things, at how often your search terms occur in the title, abstract and keywords of the article.

When you click a title, the document page will appear, with (in most cases) the abstract of the article, the author keywords, etc. After reading the abstract, you decide if you want to read the article.

Full text access

If you want to read the full article, there are different ways to get the full-text:

  • Some documents have a View PDF link, below the author information. Scopus shows this link when they know the EUR has access to the article.
  • The Full text options drop down menu shows a link to the website of the publisher and a link 'Find Full-Text @ EUR'.
  • When you are using Lean Library, you will see a pop-up when the EUR has access to the article.

Saving and exploring

  • You can save interesting and relevant articles in a list. Click the link Save to List. You need an account to use this functionality.
  • If the article has been cited by other documents in Scopus, the document page contains a 'Cited by xxx documents' box.
  • On the document page you can also find 'Related documents' within Scopus - this option searches for documents similar to the article, based on an overlap in the reference list, the keywords or the authors.

Adjusting your research question and search profile

When you have a first overview of publications that match your search profile, it is important to evaluate whether your question and the search profile require some adjustments. For example, if your search profile only returns five search results, your question may be too narrow. If, on the other hand, your first search results in more than 5,000 search results, your search was probably too broad. In the latter case you would need to further specify your research question. A good way to do this is by looking at possible interesting subtopics in your search results.

Example: The previous search profile above in the question about the role of families in eating disorders, could focus on the role of families in the treatment of eating disorders - apart from parents we also include siblings. A further possibility for specification is to have the question exclusively related to the eating disorder Anorexia Nervosa.

4. Advanced options in Scopus - impact of journal, author, and article

Scopus offers a lot of additional information about the journal an article was published in, about the author who wrote the article (what has (s)he published?) and about the article itself. These options in Scopus help you find the most important journals, authors and articles in the set of documents.

If you have found interesting subtopics or useful synonyms in the previous assignments, you might want to update your search profile and perform a new search in Scopus.

To get an overview of your topic, click Analyze results above the list of results. 'Documents by year' is the first graph shown. Scroll down the page to see the other options, for example of the documents per year by journal (source), by author, and by affiliation (the universities of the authors).

Journals

When you click 'Documents per year by source', you get an overview of the journals within your search results. Mark the three journals with the highest number of publications. Click the link Compare sources and view CiteScore, SJR, and SNIP data.

You will see a graphic presentation of the CiteScore per year for the selected journals. The CiteScore is an indicator for the citation impact of the journal. The CiteScore is calculated by the number of citations received by the journal over four years, divided by the number of documents indexed in Scopus and published in the same four years.

Other indicators in Scopus are the SJR (SCImago Journal Rank), SNIP (Source Normalized Impact per Paper), the citations by year, the number of documents, the percentage of articles not cited and the percentage of review articles (this is important because reviews get more citations than other article types). With this data you can assess the importance of a journal.

Look at the graph of the CiteScores. When you compare journals from the same subject field, you can compare the numbers you see in the graph. However, when the journals belong to different subject fields, you can't, because citation patterns differ per subject field. Therefore, a CiteScore of 10 can be the top score in one field, but only a median score in another field.

If you want to compare journals by CiteScores across fields, you have to search the journals in Scopus, by using the option Sources at the top of the screen. There you can find the CiteScore rank - that's the ranking of the journal within a subject field, based on the CiteScore. Example: the European Eating Disorders Review, using the CiteScore of 2022, is ranked 25 out of 292 journals in the subject area clinical psychology, that's the 91st percentile.

Authors

Return to the Analyze search results page. Create an overview of authors by clicking on Documents by author. Which author published the most on your topic?

When you click on the name of an author (in the left of the screen), you will see his/her author page 'Author profile' within Scopus.

Articles

  • Return to the search results. Sort by number of citations (Cited by (highest)). When searching for highly cited articles on Scopus, you'll likely find foundational or influential studies that have shaped a field over time. Are the highly cited documents relevant for your topic?
  • Next, sort by publication date (Date (newest)). Recently published articles are likely to reflect current trends and new discoveries or contributions to the debate about your topic. Are recent documents relevant for your topic?
  • Finally, limit your search results to review articles by selecting 'Review' under the 'Document type' refine option (on the left side of the screen). Again, sort by publication date (Date (newest)). Review articles synthesize existing literature and are likely to provide an overview of the current state of understanding on your topic. Are there any recent review articles relevant for your topic?

Too few, too many results?

I have too few results:

  • First check for spelling mistakes in your query.
  • Check the Boolean operators used in your query - have you used AND between the different elements of your query or also within an element?
  • Check the articles that are relevant for your topic: can you find synonyms or related terms to expand your search?

I have too many results:

  • If you use terms like positive psychology or smoking cessation, you can put them between quotation marks ("). This will make the search more specific - the terms have to be next to each other in the title, abstract or keywords.
  • Check the search results for subtopics - maybe you can add an extra element to your research question and focus for example on a particular setting, therapy or population group.
  • Check the subject area limit: there you can exclude results from irrelevant subject areas.
5. Selection of sources

After having performed your topic search in Scopus, you have to select the sources that you would like to use in your report.

Read the abstracts of the articles you have added to My List to make a judgement on the usefulness of these articles. Select a number of articles that highlight different perspectives on your subject. It is, for example, good to select articles written by different authors.

Globally read the articles that seem relevant and determine the research question, hypotheses, research method, and conclusions of the article.

Determine whether the articles are relevant to your research question.

Criteria to assess found literature

Use these questions to quickly assess whether a source is useful for your report.

  • Complete picture of the subject
    • Does the article provide information relevant to your research question?
    • Which aspect of your research question does this article address?
    • Which aspects of the question remain unanswered?
  • Where can you use the source
    • Introduction
    • Main body (which sub topic does it support?)
    • Discussion
  • Relation between articles
    • Addition does it add new evidence or perspective?
    • Replication does it confirm previous findings?
    • Contradiction does it challenge other studies?
  • Use of the articles
    • Do you need to use the entire source?
    • Which parts of the article are relevant?

Using GenAI in AWSI

Translating Ideas into Search Strings

If you want to use GenAI, you can use it to translate your research idea into a structured Scopus search string. The primary aim of this activity is to develop your familiarity with Scopus syntax. At the same time, it is an exercise in structuring your thinking. You are not simply typing a sentence into a search bar; you are breaking down an idea into clearly defined concepts and combining them using precise Boolean logic.

Students often make predictable mistakes at this stage. Ideas are entered as full sentences. Concepts are not separated properly. AND and OR are used inconsistently. Search results are accepted without examining how the query was constructed. This activity is designed to slow that process down. The goal is to construct a deliberate and logically coherent search string.

The immediate objective is to produce a well structured search string. However, the broader objective is to understand how search logic works and to develop a more intentional way of working with Scopus. The quality of your literature selection in the next stage depends directly on the clarity of your search construction.

In this process, GenAI can function as a coach or critical conversation partner. It can help you refine concepts, test alternative combinations, and identify gaps in your logic. It should not generate the final answer for you, but rather support the development of your reasoning.

To guide this process, preset prompts are provided below. Use them as a framework to structure your interaction with GenAI and to make your search logic explicit. When you use GenAI, make sure to critically evaluate the output. Check if the search string is correctly structured and if it captures the nuances of your research question. Use it as a tool to enhance your understanding of how to effectively search in Scopus, rather than as a shortcut to bypass the critical thinking process.
